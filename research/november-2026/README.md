# Consequences of the November 2026 proofs of Dirichlet GRH and VP ≠ VNP

Euler’s 65 idoneal numbers exhaust the classical list by Dirichlet GRH and earlier number theory. The smallest uniform first term for representing the other positive integers through 100 million is 14. For arbitrary inputs the frequency of needing a larger first term admits a sieve bound, and the failures at first term one have an exact classification. On the complexity side the formal permanent lower bound gives a superpolynomial gate lower bound for exact hafnians even on positive-definite correlation matrices with positive entries.

## Euler’s list and a constructive witness

In the Formal Conjectures statement a positive integer $n$ is idoneal precisely when it has no triple $0<a<b<c$ with $n=ab+bc+ca$. Weinberger’s hypothesis concerns the quadratic Dirichlet characters relevant to imaginary quadratic fields. Kani’s Corollary 23 eliminates every additional idoneal number under that hypothesis. The proved Dirichlet GRH supplies it. The published Lean project by Senamakel and collaborators records its finite checks using `native_decide` and publishes the resulting axiom reports. [Catalogue statement](https://github.com/google-deepmind/formal-conjectures/blob/9097e2f90e66d2f6244209cb1c07f7120d17ec53/FormalConjectures/Wikipedia/IdonealCompleteness.lean) · [Weinberger](https://matwbn.icm.edu.pl/ksiazki/aa/aa22/aa2221.pdf) · [Kani, p. 209](https://mast.queensu.ca/~kani/papers/2011K1.pdf) · [Prior Lean proof](https://github.com/senamakel/weinberger-grh)

The source project also used the factorization of $n+a^2$ in its elementary reduction. The compact proof below independently formalizes that known identity.

To construct the triple, write $n+a^2=(a+b)(a+c)=de$. The ordering becomes $2a<d<e$. Conversely, a divisor $d$ in that window gives $b=d-a$ and $c=(n+a^2)/d-a$. The strict inequalities imply $3a^2<n$ and $d^2<n+a^2$. These bounds make the search finite. For 1,849 the search gives $(1,4,369)$, and for 2,026 it gives $(2,3,404)$. The largest exception, 1,848, has no triple. The local Lean audit checked the equivalence and both strict bounds. [Factor-window proof](IdonealFactorWindow.lean) · [Witness finder and independent progression sieve](idoneal.py)

## The sharp first-term bound through 100,000,000

For every $1\le n\le100{,}000{,}000$ outside Euler’s list, there is a representation $n=ab+bc+ca$ with $0<a<b<c$ and $a\le14$. The uniform bound **14 is best possible**. At $a\le13$ the only uncovered integer outside Euler’s list is **136,173**. Allowing $a=14$ gives $(a,b,c)=(14,17,4385)$, and $238+74{,}545+61{,}390=136{,}173$.

The finite proof enumerates arithmetic progressions. For fixed $a,b$, the represented numbers are precisely $ab+(a+b)(b+1+j)$ for integers $j\ge0$. For each $a$ the first value increases with $b$, so the sieve can stop as soon as the first value exceeds the endpoint. After marking all progressions with $a\le13$, 66 positive integers remain. Sixty-five belong to Euler’s list. After $a=14$, exactly those 65 remain. As an independent check on the exceptional input, if $a\le13$ and $d=a+b$, the factor-window proof forces $d<370$. All $14\times370$ pairs, including harmless zero entries, can therefore be checked directly. Lean proved that 14 is the least first term for this input, using only `propext` and `Quot.sound`. [Lean proof of sharpness](SharpIdoneal.lean) · [Clean build and independent reruns](https://github.com/DomTheDeveloper/open-math-agents/actions/runs/45975796372). A second implementation enumerated the factors $d,e$ and marked a packed bitset. It reproduced the exact sets of 66 and 65 missing integers for the two cutoffs. ## How often a larger first term is needed

Let $E_A(X)$ count positive $n\le X$ for which no representation has first term $1\le a\le A$. This count includes Euler’s 65 exceptions when they lie in the interval.

**An exact formula for the first term one.** For every positive $n$, put $m=n+1$. There is no representation with $a=1$ precisely when $m$ is prime, twice a prime, the square of a prime, 8, or 16. The identity $m=(b+1)(c+1)$ reduces the test to the existence of a factorization $m=de$ with $3\le d<e$.

To see that the list is exhaustive, take a prime divisor $p$ and write $m=pq$. If $p>2$, the factors $p,q$ work whenever $q>2$ and $q\ne p$. The remaining possibilities give a prime, twice a prime, or a prime square. If $p=2$, remove the cases $q=1$ and $q$ prime. Write the composite $q=uv$ with $u,v\ge2$. If one factor is two, say $u=2$, then $m=4v$. The pair $4,v$ works except at $v=2,4$, which give 8 and 16. If $u,v>2$, the two candidate pairs $(u,2v)$ and $(v,2u)$ cannot both have equal entries. Conversely, none of the five stated types has distinct factors both at least three.

Write $\pi(x)$ for the number of primes up to $x$. For integer $N\ge15$ and $M=N+1$, the exact count is

$$E_1(N)=\pi(M)+\pi(\lfloor M/2\rfloor)+\pi(\lfloor\sqrt M\rfloor)+1.$$

Only 4 occurs in two prime families, and 8 and 16 add two separate values. At $N=100{,}000{,}000$ the answer is **8,763,819**. Lean checked the classification for every positive integer with only `propext`, `Classical.choice`, and `Quot.sound`. The independent program compared the two tests integer by integer through 100 million. By the prime number theorem, $E_1(X)\sim 3X/(2\log X)$. [All-integers Lean classification](FirstTermOne.lean) · [Exact counting check](first_term_count.py) · [Successful Lean build and 100-million-input rerun](https://github.com/DomTheDeveloper/open-math-agents/actions/runs/45978733406)

**An unconditional bound for every fixed cutoff.** For each fixed positive integer $A$,

$$E_A(X)\ll_A \frac{X}{(\log X)^A}.$$

For $\sqrt X<n\le X$ put $z=X^{1/10}$. If a prime $2A<p\le z$ divides $n+a^2$ for some $1\le a\le A$, take $d=p$ and $e=(n+a^2)/p$. Then $2a<d<e$, since $p^2\le X^{1/5}<n$. This supplies the missing representation. Every surviving $n$ must therefore avoid the $A$ residues $-1^2,\ldots,-A^2$ modulo every such prime. They are distinct because $p>2A$ and $a^2-b^2=(a-b)(a+b)$.

Apply the dimension-$A$ Selberg upper-bound sieve on this integer interval. For squarefree $d$ built from these primes the Chinese remainder theorem gives $A^{\omega(d)}$ forbidden combinations, so the count in their union differs from the interval length times $A^{\omega(d)}/d$ by $O(A^{\omega(d)})$. This gives level $D=X^{1/3}>z^2$ with a power-saving remainder, since every fixed divisor-function weight times $A^{\omega(d)}$ is $O_{A,\epsilon}(d^\epsilon)$. The sieve bound is $O_A(X\prod_{2A<p\le z}(1-A/p))$, which is $O_A(X/(\log X)^A)$ by Mertens’ estimate. The discarded $n\le\sqrt X$ fit inside this bound. In particular **the number of integers up to $X$ whose minimum first term is exactly two is asymptotic to $3X/(2\log X)$**, by subtracting $E_2(X)$ from $E_1(X)$. This argument also works independently of the Riemann hypothesis. [Koukoulopoulos, Theorem 21.2 and its hypotheses](https://dms.umontreal.ca/~koukoulo/documents/publications/primes.pdf)

**A conditional answer about all possible minima.** Dickson’s prime-tuples conjecture implies that every positive integer $k$ occurs infinitely often as the *minimum* first term. For $k=1$ take $b=2$ and vary $c>2$. Fix $k\ge2$ and choose a prime $q>2k$. Impose $n\equiv-k^2\pmod q$, $n\equiv1\pmod4$, and, for each odd prime $p\le2k$, choose a residue of $n$ for which $-n$ is a quadratic nonresidue modulo $p$. The Chinese remainder theorem gives an arithmetic progression $n=r+Wt$ with $W=4q\prod_{3\le p\le2k,\,p\ \mathrm{prime}}p$.

For $1\le a<k$ put $c_a=2$ for odd $a$ and $c_a=1$ for even $a$. The integer linear forms $(r+Wt+a^2)/c_a$ have positive leading coefficients. They are admissible. At 2 they are odd. At each odd prime dividing $W$ they are nonzero by the nonresidue choice or, at $q$, because $q$ divides neither $a-k$ nor $a+k$. Every other prime exceeds $2k$ and the $k-1$ forms exclude at most $k-1$ residues. Distinct forms are nonproportional since the shifts $a^2$ differ. Dickson’s conjecture would make them simultaneously prime for infinitely many positive $t$. At each such $n$, every $n+a^2$ for $a<k$ is prime or twice a prime, so it has no suitable factor pair. For large $t$, the factor $q$ of $n+k^2$ does give a pair with $2k<q<(n+k^2)/q$. This proves the claimed conditional result. The unconditional statements above use only the prime number theorem and the upper-bound sieve in their respective counting steps. [Green and Tao, introduction](https://annals.math.princeton.edu/wp-content/uploads/annals-v171-n3-p08-p.pdf)

## Extended RH for abelian fields

For an abelian number field $K/\mathbb Q$, the meromorphic function $\zeta_K(s)$ equals a finite product of primitive Dirichlet $L$-functions, including the Riemann zeta function for the trivial character. Every factor is holomorphic in the open critical strip. A zero of the product is a zero of at least one factor, and Dirichlet GRH puts its real part at $1/2$. This proves the stated result for every finite abelian extension, including every quadratic and every cyclotomic field. [Sharifi, Proposition 4.1.21](https://www.math.ucla.edu/~sharifi/notes/iwasawa-ch04.html) · [Previously verified unconditional Lean theorems](https://github.com/senamakel/abelian-erh)

## A divisor-sum inequality for every positive odd integer

For coprime $q$ and $n$ with $qn>5040$, multiplicativity gives $\sigma(q)\sigma(n)=\sigma(qn)<e^\gamma qn\log\log(qn)$. For odd $n$, take $q=8192=2^{13}$. Then $qn>5040$ for every positive input and $\sigma(q)=16383$. This gives the single uniform inequality in the table. The Lean proof establishes the general transfer from Robin’s inequality and the odd specialization. The unconditional instantiation imports the verified arithmetic theorem from the MIT check. ## Permanent versus determinant

Fix a field of characteristic different from two. Suppose that the $n\times n$ permanent is represented by the determinant of an $m\times m$ matrix of affine-linear forms in its $n^2$ variables. Each affine form costs $O(n^2)$ gates, including its constants, so computing all entries costs $O(m^2n^2)$. Berkowitz’s algorithm computes their determinant with $O(m^4)$ ring operations without division. Composing gives an exact circuit for the permanent of size $O(m^2n^2+m^4)$. If $m\le n^D$ for arbitrarily large $n$ at any fixed $D$, those sizes have a polynomial bound, contradicting the eventual superpolynomial permanent lower bound. Thus, for every fixed $D$, all sufficiently large $n$ require $m>n^D$. In characteristic two the generic permanent equals the determinant, so $m=n$ is available. [Circuit lower bound and field restriction](https://github.com/openai/math/blob/main/lean/docs/379.md) · [Division-free determinant complexity](https://flintlib.org/doc/gr_mat/det.html) · [Classical formulation](https://people.csail.mit.edu/sitanc/docs/gct.pdf)

## Exact hafnians on positive-definite correlation matrices

A loopless hafnian sums the product of edge weights over the perfect matchings of $2n$ vertices. For any $n\times n$ matrix $A$, only matchings that join the two vertex classes contribute to the hafnian of the symmetric block matrix below. Each such matching is indexed by one permutation. Consequently

$$\operatorname{haf}\begin{pmatrix}0&A\\A^{\mathsf T}&0\end{pmatrix}=\operatorname{per}(A).$$

Now fix all diagonal entries to one. If every independent off-diagonal entry $x_{ij}$ lies in $(0,1/(2n))$, every row’s off-diagonal sum is strictly less than one. The symmetric matrix is positive definite by strict diagonal dominance. It is therefore a correlation matrix with positive entries. Suppose a fixed exact division-free arithmetic circuit agrees with the hafnian on all these matrices. Substituting the fixed diagonal and symmetry into the circuit gives a real polynomial in the independent off-diagonal variables. Its difference from the hafnian vanishes on an open box, so it is the zero polynomial. The identity therefore holds for arbitrary real off-diagonal weights, including zeros and negative values. Substituting $A$ into the cross-block entries, and zero into the within-block entries, computes the permanent. The diagonal contributes nothing to a loopless hafnian. Substituting variables and constants preserves the gate bound in the published circuit model. A polynomial-size circuit family for this promised input set would contradict the proven permanent lower bound. In fact it inherits the eventual $n^{c(\log_2 n)^{1/3}}$ lower bound for some $c>0$, with $2n$ the hafnian matrix dimension. This is an exact nonuniform arithmetic-circuit statement. The open-box argument uses the real field.
