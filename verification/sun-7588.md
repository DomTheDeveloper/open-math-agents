# The unique failure of Sun's third A234360 conjecture through 10,000

Let $\varphi$ denote Euler's totient and put $C(n,k)=k(k+1)^{\varphi(n-k)}+1$. Zhi-Wei Sun conjectured that for every integer $n>1$ there is a $k$ with $0<k<n$ for which $C(n,k)$ is prime. Dev Pratap Singh found the first counterexample at $n=7588$. This independent computation verifies that failure and gives a prime certificate for every other integer from 2 through 10,000. [Original conjecture](https://oeis.org/A234360) · [Singh's witnesses and formalization](https://github.com/DevSingh05/oeis-a234360/tree/2dd137006c176c1b8780f50452e9a102f695d80c)

## Why every candidate at 7588 is composite

The certificate covers every integer $k$ from 1 through 7587, in order. For 6,453 candidates, a supplied integer $d$ satisfies $1<d<C(7588,k)$ and divides $C(7588,k)$. For the other 1,134, setting $m=C(7588,k)$ gives $m>2$ and $2^{m-1}\not\equiv1\pmod m$. Fermat's little theorem proves that each of those $m$ is composite. For example, $C(7588,7587)=57\,570\,157=1249\cdot46\,093$. [Witness lists in the Lean source](../proofs/OeisA234360.conjecture_3.lean) · [Exact-arithmetic checker](../tools/verify_oeis_a234360_7588.cpp)

## Why 7588 is the first failure

For each of the 7,586 integers $2\le n<7588$, the certificate records a triple $(n,k,a)$. Singh supplied the values of $k$. The independent calculation found a base $a$ and verified the following conditions on $m=C(n,k)$.

$$
a^{m-1}\equiv1\pmod m,\qquad
a^{(m-1)/q}\not\equiv1\pmod m
\quad\text{for every prime }q\mid m-1.
$$

The identity $m-1=k(k+1)^{\varphi(n-k)}$ gives all the required primes by trial division of $k$ and $k+1$. The first congruence makes $a$ a unit with order dividing $m-1$. If its order were a proper divisor, it would divide $(m-1)/q$ for some prime $q\mid m-1$, contradicting the second congruence. Thus the powers of $a$ give $m-1$ distinct units modulo $m$. Every nonzero residue is a unit, so $m$ is prime. The largest base needed across all 7,586 rows was 77. [Certificate table](../tools/sun_7588_prime_witnesses.tsv) · [Primality checker](../tools/prove_7588_minimal.cpp)

## Extending the search through 10,000

An independent search found a pair $(k,a)$ for each of the next 2,412 inputs, $7589\le n\le10000$. The same full-order test proved all 2,412 chosen values prime. The largest base in this range was 83. Together with the preceding checks, this proves that **7588 is the only counterexample for $2\le n\le10000$**. [Additional certificates](../tools/sun_after_7588_prime_witnesses.tsv)

An example shows how the same prime can certify two inputs. At $n=7342$ and at $n=9981$ the choice $k=5157$ gives the same exponent, since $\varphi(2185)=\varphi(4824)=1584$. The resulting prime $5157\cdot5158^{1584}+1$ has 19,548 bits. Lean checked the full order of base 5 and proved that this prime witnesses both inputs. [Lean proof for 7,342 and 9,981](SunLargePrime.lean)

Lean 4.33.1 checked the implication from the two modular tests to primality using Mathlib's Lucas primality theorem. It also checked directly that $k=9999$ at $n=10000$ gives the prime $99\,990\,001$. Both theorem audits listed only the three standard axioms accepted by the project. [Lean proof of the certificate criterion and endpoint](SunPrimeCertificates.lean)

## Exactly forty inputs share this prime

For fixed $k=5157$, the 19,548-bit prime above occurs for exactly **40** different inputs among all $n>5157$. The smallest is 6,836 and the largest is 11,367. Ten are beyond the exhaustive interval of this study: 10,125, 10,263, 10,383, 10,401, 10,539, 10,785, 10,953, 11,127, 11,187, and 11,367.

Equality with this prime is equivalent to $\varphi(n-5157)=1584$, since powers of 5,158 have distinct exponents. If a prime $p$ divides $m$ and $\varphi(m)$ divides 1,584, then $p-1$ divides 1,584. This leaves only 14 possible primes. A certificate lists 234 pairs $(m,\varphi(m))$. It includes $(1,1)$ and is closed under adjoining any of those primes whenever the new totient still divides 1,584. Dividing $m$ by a prime factor and using strong induction therefore proves completeness for all positive integers. Exactly 40 offsets have totient 1,584, and individual factorizations verify each one. Lean checked the equivalence, primality at every listed offset, and that the 40 offsets are distinct. [Lean proof and all offsets](SunInverseTotient.lean)

## Reproduce the checks

From the root of this revision, use a C++17 compiler with OpenMP and GMP's C++ bindings.

~~~sh
c++ -O3 -std=c++17 -fopenmp tools/verify_oeis_a234360_7588.cpp -lgmpxx -lgmp -o verify7588
OMP_NUM_THREADS=3 ./verify7588 proofs/OeisA234360.conjecture_3.lean
c++ -O3 -std=c++17 -fopenmp tools/prove_7588_minimal.cpp -lgmpxx -lgmp -o minimal
OMP_NUM_THREADS=3 ./minimal tools/sun_7588_prime_witnesses.tsv > replay.tsv
cmp tools/sun_7588_prime_witnesses.tsv replay.tsv
OMP_NUM_THREADS=3 ./minimal tools/sun_after_7588_prime_witnesses.tsv 7589 10000 > after.tsv
cmp tools/sun_after_7588_prime_witnesses.tsv after.tsv
~~~

Both exhaustive conclusions above were checked with exact integer arithmetic. A second certificate for the same 7,587 candidates replaces 131 Fermat tests with direct divisors. The local Lean 4.33.1 kernel checked all 6,584 direct divisors, 426 further cases by Fermat’s theorem, and the largest remaining modulus (65,462 bits). The upstream checker tracks the complete single-file proof. [Submitted Lean proof and verification status](https://github.com/GuillaumeBAUD-coder/open-math-agents/pull/3802)
